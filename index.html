Homeric meter guide

Reading dactylic hexameter

Each verse has six feet. Feet 1–4 can be dactyls (– ⏑ ⏑, long–short–short) or spondees (– –, long–long). The fifth is usually a dactyl. The sixth ends – ×, where × can be long or short.

How to scan a line

  1. Read across word boundaries. A foot may start or end inside a word.
  2. Mark syllables long by nature when they contain a long vowel or diphthong. A short vowel before two consonants usually gives a syllable long by position, even across words. A stop followed by a liquid can behave differently.
  3. Fit the syllables into six feet. An apostrophe can mark an elided vowel that contributes no syllable. Homer can also shorten a long vowel or diphthong before another vowel, or combine two vowel sounds into one syllable.
  4. Look for a caesura, a word break within a foot, often in the third foot. A word break at a foot boundary is a diaeresis.

Worked example · Iliad 1.1

μῆνιν ἄ | ειδε θε | ὰ ‖ Πη | ληϊά | δεω Ἀχι | λῆος

– ⏑ ⏑ | – ⏑ ⏑ | – ‖ – | – ⏑ ⏑ | – ⏑ ⏑ | – ×

The third foot crosses the word break after θεὰ (‖). In the fifth foot, εω in Πηληϊάδεω scans as one syllable (synizesis).
